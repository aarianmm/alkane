IUPAC name generator: draw a structure, get the name

The Alkane is a free IUPAC name generator. Build an organic molecule atom by atom in the editor above and its systematic name appears underneath, updating every time you change the structure. There is no sign-up, no install and no cost.

What does this tool do?

It converts a structure into a name. You draw the skeleton — carbons, heteroatoms, double and triple bonds, rings, functional groups — and the naming engine works out the parent chain, numbers it, orders the substituents and returns the IUPAC name, for example 2-methylbutane, propan-2-one or methyl 3-nitrobenzoate.

It is built for the part of chemistry that is hardest to practise alone: you can check your own answer against the machine's instantly, and change one bond to see exactly how the name changes with it.

How do you name a molecule with it?

  1. Pick an atom or a bond from the toolbar — carbon by default, or O, N, S, F, Cl, Br or I.
  2. Click a grey stub on the drawing to grow the molecule in that direction. Click an existing atom to replace it, or a bond to change its order.
  3. Add rings and groups from the Rings and Groups menus — cyclopropane through cyclooctane, benzene, and groups such as carbonyl, carboxylic acid, amide, nitrile and nitro.
  4. Read the name below the canvas. It refreshes as you draw; if a structure falls outside the supported set, the tool says so instead of guessing.

Three drawing conventions are available — displayed (every atom and bond shown), structural (condensed) and skeletal (the zig-zag line notation used in exams) — so you can practise reading the same molecule in whichever form your syllabus uses.

What molecules can it name?

The engine covers the organic nomenclature taught at school and first-year undergraduate level, with main chains of up to ten carbons:

  • Hydrocarbons — straight and branched alkanes, alkenes and alkynes, with correct chain selection and lowest-locant numbering.
  • Functional groups — carboxylic acids, acyl chlorides, amides, nitriles, aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, thiols, amines and imines, named as a suffix when they are the principal group and as a prefix when they are not.
  • Halogenoalkanes and nitro compounds — fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo and nitro prefixes, with multiplying prefixes up to nona-.
  • Rings — cyclopropane to cyclodecane, with substituents.
  • Benzene derivatives — including the retained parent names phenol, benzoic acid, benzaldehyde, benzonitrile, benzamide and benzoyl chloride.
  • Esters, ethers, amines, amides and anhydrides — such as methyl ethanoate, methoxyethane, N-methylethanamide and ethanoic anhydride.

Deliberately out of scope: fused and bridged ring systems, heterocycles, stereochemistry (E/Z, R/S), charged species, isotopes and radicals. The engine names a molecule exactly or rejects it — it never returns an approximate name, which is what makes it safe to check your homework against.

How does it decide which chain is the parent?

It applies the IUPAC selection rules in order: the longest chain wins; ties are broken by the chain carrying the most principal-group occurrences, then by the lowest locants for that group, then for double and triple bonds, then for the prefixes. Where the rules genuinely leave two names equally valid, both are shown rather than one picked arbitrarily.

Frequently asked questions

What is an IUPAC name?

An IUPAC name is the systematic name of a compound, built from rules published by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry. Where a common name like "acetone" has to be memorised, an IUPAC name like propan-2-one describes the structure itself: the length of the main chain, what is attached to it, and where.

Is The Alkane free?

Yes — free, no account, nothing to install. It runs in the browser and the naming engine is open source.

Why does it say it cannot name my molecule?

Because it names exactly or refuses. If your structure uses a feature outside the supported set — a fused ring, a heterocycle, a chain longer than ten carbons, a charge — it reports that rather than inventing a plausible-looking name.

Is there an API?

Yes. The same engine is a free public HTTP API: POST a JSON atom-and-bond list to https://api.thealkane.com/api/name and it returns the name. See the interactive API documentation.

Can I use it on a phone?

Yes. The editor is touch-friendly and the layout adapts to small screens.

Does it do stereochemistry?

No. The molecule model holds atoms and bonds but no three-dimensional arrangement, so E/Z and R/S descriptors are out of scope.
