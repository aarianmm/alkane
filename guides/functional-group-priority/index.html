Functional group priority in IUPAC names: suffix or prefix?

A molecule can carry several functional groups at once, but only one of them gets to be the suffix. This guide sets out the priority order that decides which group wins, how the rest turn into prefixes, and two full worked examples — check the same chemistry live in the namer.

What does functional group priority mean?

It means that when a molecule has more than one functional group, only the single highest-priority group present becomes the name's suffix — every other group present is demoted to a prefix, no matter how it would be named on its own.

A molecule with both an -OH and a -COOH, for instance, is never named as an "-ol" acid: the carboxylic acid outranks the alcohol, so the acid becomes the suffix and the hydroxyl becomes a hydroxy- prefix instead.

What is the IUPAC priority order?

From highest to lowest, the groups rank as follows. A group higher up the table always outranks every group below it, regardless of which one happens to appear first when you read the structure.

Group Formula Suffix when principal Prefix when not
Carboxylic acid COOH -oic acid carboxy
Anhydride CO-O-CO -oic anhydride —
Ester COO -yl -oate (two words) —
Acyl chloride COCl -oyl chloride chlorocarbonyl
Amide CONH2 -amide carbamoyl
Nitrile C≡N -nitrile cyano
Aldehyde CHO -al formyl
Ketone C=O -one oxo
Alcohol OH -ol hydroxy
Thiol SH -thiol sulfanyl
Amine NH2 -amine amino
Imine =NH -imine imino

Carbon–carbon double and triple bonds are not on this table because they never compete for the suffix position at all — they are folded straight into the parent chain's stem as the infix -en- or -yn- (pent-2-ene, hex-3-yne). Halogens (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo) and the nitro group are different again: they have no suffix form at all in this scheme and are always cited as prefixes.

Anhydrides and esters are worth a note. They sit high in the order, just below the carboxylic acid, but they are not named with an ordinary chain suffix: an ester becomes two words naming the alcohol side then the acid side (methyl ethanoate, ethyl propanoate), and an anhydride is named from its parent acid (ethanoic anhydride). The namer builds both by that two-part pattern rather than by hanging a suffix off a parent chain, which is why they behave differently from everything below them in the table.

How do you use the table to name a molecule?

Work through the table in four steps: find every functional group in the molecule, let the one that ranks highest in the table become the suffix, number the parent chain to give that suffix the lowest possible locant, then name everything else as an alphabetised prefix.

  1. Identify every functional group present in the structure.
  2. Find which one sits highest in the priority table — that one becomes the suffix.
  3. Number the chain so the suffix group gets the lowest locant available.
  4. Name every other group as a prefix, in alphabetical order, ignoring multiplying prefixes.

Worked example: a molecule with an –OH and a –COOH

Take CH3CH(OH)CH2COOH. Both a hydroxyl and a carboxylic acid are present, and the acid outranks the alcohol, so the acid is the suffix. The chain is four carbons, numbered from the acid carbon as C1 so the suffix gets the lowest possible locant — that puts the hydroxyl on C3. The alcohol is demoted to a hydroxy- prefix at that locant, giving 3-hydroxybutanoic acid.

Worked example: a ketone with a chloro substituent

Take CH3COCH2CHClCH3. The ketone is the only group in the priority table present, so it is automatically the suffix; the chlorine, having no suffix form at all, is always a prefix regardless. Numbering the five-carbon chain to give the ketone the lowest locant puts C=O at C2 and the chlorine at C4, giving 4-chloropentan-2-one.

Why are halogens never a suffix?

Halogens are never a suffix because IUPAC nomenclature never gave them one — fluoro, chloro, bromo and iodo are always cited as substituent prefixes, at whatever locant they sit, no matter what else is in the molecule. The same is true of the nitro group. This is why a chlorine substituent never bumps a ketone, an alcohol or any other group in the table out of the suffix position; it competes for locants, not for the suffix itself.

Where do the locants go?

A locant sits immediately in front of the part of the name it describes, not at the very front of the whole word — so the correct form is pentan-2-ol, not 2-pentanol, and but-2-ene, not 2-butene. Older style put every locant at the start of the name; current recommendations place each one directly before the syllable it modifies.

What about benzene rings?

On a benzene ring, a small set of retained names take over the whole parent instead of a prefix-plus-suffix construction: phenol for hydroxyl, benzoic acid for the carboxylic acid, benzaldehyde for the aldehyde, benzonitrile for the nitrile, benzamide for the amide, and benzoyl chloride for the acyl chloride. Whichever of these groups is present becomes the parent name and takes ring position 1; any other, lower-priority substituent on the ring is then cited as a prefix at its own locant, exactly as on a chain. A lower-priority group never displaces one of these retained parents.

Common mistakes

  • Naming an alcohol as the suffix when a carboxylic acid, amide or other higher-ranked group is also present.
  • Giving a halogen a suffix-like ending instead of citing it as a chloro- style prefix.
  • Putting the locant at the front of the whole name instead of directly before the part it modifies.
  • Numbering the chain for the lowest locant on a minor substituent instead of on the principal group.
  • Forgetting that a retained benzene parent (phenol, benzoic acid, and so on) takes over position 1 automatically.
